Board 06 · /progress and /settings

Trend over noise.
Never invent a number.

Two very different screens with one shared responsibility: they are where the app tells you the truth about yourself. Progress must separate signal from daily fluctuation. Settings must never put a stranger's body weight in your profile — which it currently can.

What we take from whom

  • MacroFactorThe trend line is the hero, the raw daily dots are faint context. Reviewers call the expenditure algorithm a genuine innovation, and this chart is how it becomes believable.
  • LifesumOne summary score people can hold in their head, instead of four competing metrics.
  • ResearchGoals and progress bars are the most-preferred motivational elements. Not badges, not points.
  • CronometerDetail is welcome; undifferentiated detail is not. Depth belongs one tap down.
  • MyFitnessPalThe warning: a settings screen people do not trust is a settings screen people stop opening.

Concept A — the trend, not the scale

/progress One line that means something, one number that does not

The behavioural point from your own blueprint: daily weight swings from water and digestion, and reacting to them is counterproductive. The chart's job is to make the smoothed line feel more real than this morning's reading. That is a visual hierarchy problem, not a maths problem.

  • The trend number is the big one. Today's raw weight is deliberately secondary and carries a plain-language reassurance next to it.
  • Raw readings stay visible as faint dots — hiding them entirely reads as the app hiding something.
  • The range selector drives the query. Today the page reads a user's entire weight history on every visit, one document per day, forever.
  • A designed empty state: under three weigh-ins there is no trend to draw, and the chart should say so instead of drawing a straight line between two dots.

Concept B — plan history worth revisiting

/progress Past weeks as reusable objects

The missed opportunity: plan history is currently a read-only list. But the most valuable thing about a week you already ate is that you can eat it again — and that is the fastest possible route to a full plan.

  • Reuse copies the week forward into the next empty one. One tap from history to a full plan.
  • Completion percentage is real — computed from actual isEaten ticks, not asserted. This is one of the few numbers on the page that is already honest.
  • Say the cap out loud. The page currently reads every plan you have ever saved and silently shows eight. Either page properly or state the limit.
  • Fix the dates. Ranges currently render one day early for anyone west of Greenwich, so History and Planner disagree about the same week.

Concept C — settings that never guess

/settings Empty means empty

The most severe finding in the whole audit lives here. A new account opens Settings and sees a complete profile — 185 lb current, 170 lb goal, 2,200 kcal — none of which the user entered. Change your dietary tags, press Save, and you have silently adopted a stranger's body. Every other invented value in the app merely displays fiction. This one writes it.

What Settings already gets right

The draft-and-commit model is careful and worth preserving: edits live in a local draft, the store cannot overwrite work in progress, Discard genuinely resets, and a failed save keeps the draft and says so. That is better than most of the apps studied. The only change needed is one blocking alert layered on top of an already-good inline message.

Where this all points

Progress is where the Adaptive Coaching Engine will eventually live — the weekly moment where logged intake plus weight trend produce a new target. Every concept on this board is shaped so that when the engine exists, it slots into the top of Progress and the top of the Dashboard without a redesign: the trend chart is already the hero, the honest-progress card is already in place, and neither screen is currently claiming the engine is running.
